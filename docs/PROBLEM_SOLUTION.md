# Mission Alive | Problem and solution

## The problem

Space science already has wonder built into it, yet young learners often meet it as a collection of separate articles, images, and numbers. At ages 10 to 14, ideas such as signal delay, robotic autonomy, thin atmospheres, infrared light, and interstellar distance are difficult to grasp through explanation alone. They become meaningful when a learner can make a choice, see what happens, and revise that choice.

Recent education data gives this challenge added urgency. The average science score for United States eighth-grade students in 2024 was four points lower than in 2019. Earth and Space Sciences also fell by four points, with declines across all five reported performance percentiles. These results do not identify a single cause, but they do show a need for more engaging ways to practise scientific thinking. [Source: 2024 NAEP Grade 8 Science, National Trends](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/reports/science/2024/national-trends/)

Access is part of the same problem. The World Health Organization estimates that 1.3 billion people, about 16 percent of the global population, experience significant disability. A learning experience that depends on sound, color, precise dragging, long passages of text, or a working 3D renderer can exclude students before the science begins. [Source: World Health Organization, Disability](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health)

NASA missions provide remarkable stories, but their lessons are spread across mission pages, archives, datasets, photographs, and technical reports. Ingenuity, for example, was designed for no more than five test flights in 30 days. It completed 72 flights over nearly three years. That is a memorable lesson about testing, adaptation, and engineering courage, especially when a learner gets to experience the cycle instead of only reading the final number. [Source: NASA/JPL-Caltech, The Legacy of Ingenuity](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/the-legacy-of-ingenuity-the-frontier-of-extraterrestrial-flight/)

The challenge is therefore not a lack of information. The challenge is turning trustworthy space science into an experience that children can understand, control, remember, and access in different ways.

## The solution

Mission Alive is an interactive space academy for learners aged 10 to 14. Six machines become teachers because they cannot come home: the Apollo 17 Lunar Roving Vehicle, Surveyor 3, Curiosity, Ingenuity, Webb, and Voyager 1.

Each machine gives the learner a job connected to its real mission. Students repair and test a lunar rover fender, investigate fictional soil evidence before a landing, program or drive a Mars rover, manage a helicopter flight, unfold and align a space telescope, and answer the Golden Record with a greeting of their own.

The science is carried by action. Curiosity teaches that evidence of habitability is not proof of life. NASA describes it as a mission seeking the conditions that could have supported microbial life, not a life-detection mission. [Source: NASA, The Science of Curiosity](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/the-science-of-curiosity-seeking-signs-of-past-mars-habitability/)

Webb turns an engineering constraint into something a learner can manipulate. Its primary mirror has 18 segments, and two side wings were folded so the observatory could fit inside its launch vehicle. In the challenge, students unfold the wings and bring scattered star images into focus. [Source: NASA, Webb's Mirrors](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/webbs-mirrors/)

Voyager adds a human dimension. Its Golden Record carries greetings in 55 languages. Visitors can hear or read one of those greetings, then write or record a response without being forced to grant microphone access. [Source: NASA, Golden Record Greetings](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/greetings/)

Progress is framed as one journey rather than six unrelated activities. Every completed mission earns a stamp. Collecting all six opens a graduation finale and printable certificate. First-person transmissions give each machine a voice, while a mission passport gives children a reason to continue.

Failure is designed to teach. A wrong route, unstable landing, weak repair, or misaligned image produces a specific next step. Learners can retry quickly and usually succeed after two or three informed attempts. The goal is not to punish mistakes. It is to make revision feel like real engineering.

Official material keeps the experience grounded. Mission Alive uses credited NASA and STScI imagery, NASA's 72-flight Ingenuity log, the 55 Golden Record greetings, JPL Horizons estimates, and carefully checked Deep Space Network states. Simulations are labelled as teaching models rather than NASA measurements.

Accessibility shapes the mechanics from the beginning. The academy supports keyboard and touch input, reduced motion, sound-off play, visible focus, text alternatives, a 2D fallback, and microphone-free completion. English, Spanish, and Vietnamese interfaces are bundled locally. No account is required for the core journey.

Mission Alive asks more than, "What did this machine accomplish?" It lets a child confront a smaller version of the problem that made the accomplishment meaningful.

## One-paragraph submission version

Space missions contain powerful lessons, but young learners often encounter them as disconnected facts and images. Mission Alive turns six real machines into an accessible interactive academy for ages 10 to 14. Students repair Apollo 17's rover, investigate lunar soil with Surveyor 3, program Curiosity, fly Ingenuity, align Webb, and answer Voyager's Golden Record. Each challenge combines sourced space science, visual onboarding, useful failure feedback, and a certification stamp. Real NASA and STScI media, mission archives, JPL Horizons estimates, and Deep Space Network data keep the experience grounded, while keyboard controls, reduced motion, optional sound, a 2D fallback, and English, Spanish, and Vietnamese interfaces widen participation. After six stamps, learners graduate with a printable certificate and a clearer sense that science advances through evidence, iteration, and human curiosity.

## Tóm tắt tiếng Việt

Kiến thức không gian vốn hấp dẫn, nhưng học sinh thường tiếp cận nó qua những bài viết, hình ảnh và con số tách rời. Mission Alive biến sáu cỗ máy thành sáu người hướng dẫn trong một học viện không gian tương tác. Trẻ sửa xe Apollo 17, khảo sát đất với Surveyor 3, lập trình Curiosity, điều khiển Ingenuity, căn chỉnh Webb và gửi lời chào cùng Voyager. Mỗi nhiệm vụ kết hợp kiến thức có nguồn, thử thách có thể sửa sai, phản hồi rõ ràng và một con dấu chứng nhận. Trải nghiệm hỗ trợ bàn phím, cảm ứng, reduced motion, chế độ 2D, chơi không âm thanh và ba ngôn ngữ. Sau sáu con dấu, học sinh tốt nghiệp với chứng chỉ có thể in và một bài học chung: khoa học tiến lên nhờ bằng chứng, thử nghiệm và sự kiên trì.
